# Wide rendering integration plan

This plan records completed experimental and migration slices. Current implementation and verification are tracked in [wide rendering cleanup](wide-rendering-cleanup.md) and the [implemented architecture map](../../docs/interactive-rendering-architecture.md). ## Historical scope and decisions

The work began as disposable experimentation to establish whether and how wide execution improved speed through the real worker. Formal temporary-CPU bounds and production admission were outside that experiment. Explicitly enabled builds, bounded admission and external process-memory guards provided coarse safeguards. Ordinary Cargo defaults stayed unchanged. The owner later approved automatic concurrency within the experiment build.

The owner selected one execution engine for all workloads, including width one, and cache memory as the user-facing resource control. Automatic concurrency replaced the separate width preference. Evaluation distinguished first-feedback from complete-set latency and attributed material differences without requiring exact ownership attribution of insignificant memory deltas.

The owner selected roughly 1 GB of render-cache capacity per simultaneously edited Speed photograph as the initial sizing heuristic, corresponding to an 8 GB setting for eight photographs. The native-cache rebuild measurements motivated that target without establishing a supported recommendation across representative edits, zoom regions and shared/temporary overhead. The [resource contract](../../docs/specs/render-orchestration.md#reuse-and-resource-ownership) records its limits.

Owner testing exposed costly native-region preparation, repeated cold native caches and later full-native Current rendering. The owner selected the [priority contract](../../docs/specs/render-orchestration.md#foreground-and-speculative-execution): viewport → navigator/scopes → browser thumbnails → idle full-image continuations, with a persistent background-completion toggle for battery use.

Two owner captures cover six zoomed Merrill photos with a 6,000 MiB cache target. The recording ending at maximum width four is complete and loss-free. The other ends at width six but overwrites older ring-buffer data, so its missing early history is not used to prove absence of work.

## What the evidence supports

The current [experiment report](../../docs/wide-rendering-experiments.md) finds little group-latency benefit from round-robin graph traversal with existing operation waits. Bounded completion grouping improves group time. Within-photo batching and completion polling also matter. Wider execution can delay the first result. The owner considers the measured warm case's approximately 5 ms first-image cost worthwhile for approximately 49 ms saved across the set. This favors useful group throughput when memory permits. Larger-grid, cold and actual-input latency gates still need review.

The selected mechanism used independently owned photo executions feeding a bounded window of submitted GPU work. The GPU determined physical overlap. CPU threads, GPU submissions and shader dispatch packing were separate choices. A same-stage barrier was unnecessary and could block useful work when recipes or cache hits differed.

## Agreed starting approach

The owner initially selected a private core job driver after comparing it with persistent jobs owned directly by Engine. The application worker remained the adapter to coordinator demand and delivery. The early sketch placed admitted-job state and bounded completion windows in the driver, using engine operations and existing session/resource accounting. Individual executions owned resumable state while retained per-photo sessions and caches outlived them. Later implementation and closing reviews retained worker-owned admitted executions and working-set-owned sessions.

Names and the public interface were provisional at that stage. The choice did not require a separate service thread, a stateless Engine or a new object for every responsibility.

The owner required a closing ownership review against implemented call paths, validation and feedback rather than preserving the initial sketch. Its findings are recorded below, and the final shared-execution review is recorded in the active cleanup plan.

## Early ownership and execution design

The initial design separated coordinator demand and settlement, the worker's single service cursor and verified-source lookup, per-image resumable execution, working-set session protection, and engine/GPU resources. It used one CPU orchestration thread. Graph/session borrows stayed local rather than creating self-referential jobs. Completed-window delivery drained submitted work before a bounded reply handoff could block. Per-job calibration and diagnostic context were applied locally to avoid inheriting a peer's settings.

The early cancellation callback also selected per-operation waits. That coupling was identified as migration work so fresh CLI/export execution could retain queued allocation overlap while interactive and grouped work kept their completion boundaries. The final cleanup made completion policy explicit. Submission completion callbacks covered the queue prefix at registration, including racing UI work. Guarded scratch reuse relied on ordered submissions rather than a new fence for every reuse.

The inventory design distinguished deduplicated retained inputs, preparation, live graph values, scratch, outputs, pending delivery and display resources from within-operation temporaries, allocator effects and driver overhead. The allocation-traffic threshold was not a residency budget. Experimental coarse headroom and mandatory over-budget width-one progress did not establish strict temporary or process-memory bounds.

The real-worker experiment covered widths one, two, four and eight plus an ordinary serial control. Eight scale-8 and two scale-2 cohorts passed exact complete-image comparisons. Separate phase memory and a loss-free width-eight GPU capture were recorded in the [experiment report](../../docs/wide-rendering-experiments.md#real-worker-experiment). Independent review supported the common-engine direction and identified duplicated preparation and nested single-job wrappers for later removal. Those measurements alone did not establish controlled replacement/backpressure coverage, native presentation or production admission.

The width-one extraction preserved operation waits and one dispatcher. All 144 maintained camera/resolution/policy cases matched complete serial samples and geometry, and four focused GPU tests passed. Independent reviews covered extraction and session protection. ### Closing audit findings and behavioral evidence

Independent core and GUI reviews recommended retaining worker-owned admitted executions and working-set-owned sessions. They found no evidence that another scheduler, an Engine-owned session manager or a universal job framework would simplify the design. The numerical dispatcher was already shared. The audit identified duplication around preparation, completion, cache settlement and artifact handoff.

At that audit checkpoint, the worker distinguished warm grouped rendering from a synchronous fallback covering presentation reuse, initial source deferral and genuinely unsupported numerical work. `Photos::render_reduced` combined recovery/sealing with synchronous rendering, and the worker carried a parallel precomputed-result tuple into its ordinary render closure. The audit selected reuse of the completed-job handoff and priority-attempt helper rather than another preparation framework.

The independent stress agent ran six previously unselected GPU cases and eight coordinator/runtime cases against the frozen reviewed build. All 14 passed under their declared source contracts and a 4 GiB sampled process guard. The highest observed process footprint was about 3.46 GiB. These cover sequential mixed native/reduced history, selection/clear, cancellation after sealing, grid promotion, source replacement and bounded delivery. They do not establish simultaneous mixed-source behavior.

Two behavioral tests added reduced Speed chroma coverage. A/B submitted one grouped window before a third viewport target arrived. A cached rotated C reused photographic pixels with zero photographic dispatches and no source reloads. A cold C was held through the existing source-acquisition control while A/B finished, then completed after release. Both cases settled every latest target with exact fresh samples/settings/geometry and preserved warm source identities. Independent test review and guarded debug runs passed.

Those initial tests observed work without requiring efficient scheduling: the A/B-only control admitted two jobs and used six two-peer windows. Each arrival case admitted four warm-job attempts and used seven two-peer windows. In the cached case, aggregate dispatches rose from 14 to 17, comprising one necessary C display dispatch and two extra peer dispatches. This proved retirement/re-admission work, not a material latency regression or native-region behavior. Aggregate snapshots included baseline validation readbacks, so those readback counts were not timed-work observations. Dispatch counts and per-window stamp records established the extra work later targeted by cleanup.

The audit also found older native session runners serving inspection/comparison, picking, photographic export/batch and maintained profiling tools. A direct reduced-session runner served idle prefetch, while fresh CLI rendering/profiling used a separate recipe execution/collection chain. Reduced seal/refresh and DNG writing had necessary transaction/output differences, without requiring separate photographic schedulers.

The audit identified an important preservation constraint: stateless rendering queued to an allocation boundary, while cancellable session rendering waited per operation. ### Historical allocation-audit hypothesis

An independent source audit suggested a smaller target than the complete reconstruction pipeline. Warm neutral-detail chroma changes could reuse upstream preparation and require `Texture → TiledColor → TiledColorDq → ProjectColorTiles → ResampleRgb → Tone`, connected by zero-copy aliases. This was a source-derived explanation for the observed six graph dispatches plus presentation per photo, not a verified allocation or admission bound.

The proposed audit scope was nonspatial Texture producing layers, final photographic output, no region and profiling Off. It excluded unknown operations, extra roots, spatial Texture and cold reconstruction. Tone-only exposure could exercise the lifecycle without establishing warm-reconstruction performance.

The hypothesis associated allocation formulas with [texture](../../crates/foveonic/src/nodes/color/texture.rs), [reconstruction](../../crates/foveonic/src/nodes/color/reconstruction.rs), [resize](../../crates/foveonic/src/nodes/finish/resize.rs) and tone helpers. Its conservative fresh-output subtotal for the six-operation chain was `36A + 12P + 24Q` bytes, where A was atlas pixels, P projected pixels and Q photographic pixels. Aligned tables, uniforms and future presentation storage, including `16V` display bytes for V view pixels, were additional charges. Summing remaining allocations was simpler to audit than optimized liveness simulation but could deny useful width two conservatively.

The audit identified distinct accounting lifetimes: actual session/cursor/scratch/frontend allocations counted once, fresh outputs transferred from estimates to actual owners, and uploaded uniforms/tables and obsolete submitted work charged until retirement. A fence reconciled those ownership transitions.

A GPU whitelist alone was insufficient because the prepared recipe owned the whole graph, including cached nodes and heap payloads. Settings, cursor arrays, nested read lists, graph containers and shared tables needed retained-owner accounting. CPU preparation, future display/delivery and Wall/GPU observation/query/readback bounds were outside the width-one foundation. Current retained accounting and the unresolved strict temporary/production bounds are described by the architecture map, cleanup plan and durable specifications.
